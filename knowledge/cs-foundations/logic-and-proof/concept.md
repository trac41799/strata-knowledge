# Logic & Proof

## Claims

- Propositional logic is the logic of statements: formulas are built from atoms and the connectives ¬ (not), ∧ (and), ∨ (or), → (implies); a formula is valid (a tautology) when it is true under every truth assignment and satisfiable when true under at least one [T0][S-0043].
- First-order logic extends propositional logic with terms, predicates, equality, and the quantifiers ∀ and ∃ over individuals; it is strictly more expressive — propositional logic has no way to state claims like "every natural number has a successor" [T0][S-0043].
- Validity and entailment are semantic notions: Γ entails φ (Γ ⊨ φ) when every model of Γ is a model of φ; provability (Γ ⊢ φ) is the syntactic counterpart, generated by a proof system's rules [T0][S-0043].
- Gödel's completeness theorem (1930): in first-order logic every valid formula is provable, so semantic validity and syntactic derivability coincide — Γ ⊨ φ if and only if Γ ⊢ φ [T0][S-0043].
- Soundness is the other direction of the same coincidence: every provable formula is valid, so a sound proof system never derives a formula false in some model; together, soundness and completeness make "provable" and "valid" two views of one set [T0][S-0043].
- Compactness (a corollary of completeness): a set of first-order formulas is satisfiable iff every finite subset is satisfiable — any inconsistency has a finite witness [T0][S-0043].
- Propositional validity is decidable by truth tables, and completeness ties this semantic check to provability; for first-order validity no such decision procedure exists (Entscheidungsproblem — see cs-foundations/computability) [T0][S-0043].
- Natural deduction (Gentzen 1935) is a proof system with one introduction and one elimination rule per connective — ∧-intro/∧-elim, →-intro, ∨-elim (proof by cases) — designed to mirror informal mathematical reasoning [T0][S-0044].
- Assumptions and their discharge are first-class in natural deduction: a derivation of B under assumption A licenses the inference A → B by →-introduction — exactly what a direct proof does [T0][S-0044].
- The classical system is natural deduction plus the law of excluded middle (equivalently double-negation elimination ¬¬P ⊢ P); without it the system is intuitionistic logic, where excluded middle, double-negation elimination, and proof by contradiction are not available [T0][S-0044].
- Natural deduction in both variants is sound and complete for first-order logic; Gentzen's same 1935 paper proves the Hauptsatz for his sequent calculus — every derivation normalizes to a cut-free one — yielding consistency and founding modern proof normalization [T0][S-0044].
- The Peano axioms (Peano 1889) are the first explicit axiomatization of the natural numbers: a first element, an injective successor function, and the induction axiom — any property holding of the first element and closed under successor holds of every natural number [T0][S-0045].
- Proof by mathematical induction applies the induction axiom: to prove ∀n P(n), prove the base case P(0) (or P(1)) and the inductive step ∀k (P(k) → P(k+1)); the step must be established for an arbitrary k, not for any particular value [T0][S-0045].
- Structural induction generalizes the principle to recursively defined structures (lists, trees, formulas, terms): prove the property for each constructor, assuming it holds for the subcomponents — valid because such structures are generated by well-founded constructor chains [T0][S-0045].
- Standard proof strategies are natural deduction rules read as tactics: direct proof (→-introduction), proof by cases (∨-elimination), proof by contradiction (¬-introduction plus classical double-negation elimination), and proof by contrapositive (classical) [T0][S-0044].
- Proof by contradiction is valid in classical logic but non-constructive: it establishes a conclusion without exhibiting a witness or a direct argument; intuitionistic logic does not admit it in general [T0][S-0044].

## Details

- Truth tables decide propositional validity, but the table for n atoms has 2^n rows — a simple decision procedure, not an efficient one; the exponential cost is where complexity theory begins [T0][S-0043].
- The completeness theorem guarantees a proof exists for every valid formula but provides no method to find it; proof search for first-order logic is undecidable in general [T0][S-0043].
- Soundness is the non-negotiable property of any mechanized formal system: a single unsound rule lets a system derive invalid formulas, and the underlying logic's completeness does not repair that [T0][S-0043].

## Boundaries / common misunderstandings

- Soundness and completeness are separate directions: a proof system can be sound but incomplete (some valid formulas unprovable); the completeness of first-order logic is a substantial theorem, not a definition [T0][S-0043].
- "Complete" means every valid formula has a proof; it does not mean proofs can be found mechanically, and it does not make "provable" and "true" synonymous outside this validity semantics — soundness and completeness concern the relation between proofs and the first-order validity semantics only [T0][S-0043].
- Proof by contradiction is a classical-law move (¬¬P ⊢ P), not a universal rule of all logics; it is also not the same as circular reasoning — assuming the conclusion is invalid in every system [T0][S-0044].
- Induction does not "prove each case as you go": a finite list of verified instances never establishes ∀n P(n), and a missing base case can make an induction "proof" accept a false claim (e.g., the all-horses-are-the-same-color fallacy) [T0][S-0045].
- Validity and satisfiability are different properties: a formula can be satisfiable but invalid (true in some models, false in others); proving a theorem means establishing validity, not finding one satisfying assignment [T0][S-0043].
